"You are fluent enough in the statistics to be a real partner to data scientists. You can tell the difference between a result that is weak and a result that is invalid."

My outbound system runs a documented split test, and the protocol was written before any data came in. Verbatim from my own operating canon: every batch of 20 or more splits 50/50 between two message frameworks, same audience segment, same send window, same prospect quality, with each message tagged to its arm. Tracked metrics are open, reply, positive reply and booking. The decision rule was fixed in advance: whichever framework wins by 30% or more on positive-reply lift becomes the default, reviewed at a fixed four-week date.

I want to be precise about what that is and is not. It is a real design: matched assignment, a held window, one primary metric chosen up front, and a pre-committed threshold so I could not talk myself into a favorable read afterward. It is not a platform, and the sample sizes are outbound-marketing sized, not million-patient sized.

The instinct underneath it is the one you're describing. I wrote the threshold down first specifically because I did not trust myself to look at the numbers and be honest afterward.

Threats to validity

No power analysis was run, so a 30% lift threshold on batches of 20 or more is a business rule rather than a statistically derived one. There is no correction for the four metrics tracked. And I designed and judged this myself, with no data scientist to argue with me, which is exactly the check your role would put on me.

"You have debugged the hard class of measurement bugs: traffic that split unevenly, users who ended up in both arms, effects that vanished once you looked at who was actually exposed, identity that broke between anonymous and logged-in sessions."

I am not going to dress this one up. I have not debugged an uneven traffic split or a both-arms bug, because I have not run a system where those were possible. If that is the load-bearing requirement, I am the wrong candidate and you should stop here.

What I would offer instead is that I have debugged the structurally identical bug in a different costume. My automation once sent the same person the same message twice, which is a double-exposure bug: the same subject counted twice because identity was resolved per code path instead of centrally. The fix was not a patch in one path. It was a single deterministic guard keyed on the recipient that reads the live sent record and fails closed when it cannot read it, with every outbound path routed through it.

That is the same lesson your bullet is pointing at: the trustworthiness of a number is decided by the weakest link in the chain that produces it, and the only durable fix is one central place that owns identity. I learned it on a smaller stage and I would rather tell you where I learned it than imply I learned it at yours.

Threats to validity

An analogy is not experience. Recipient-keyed deduplication in an outbound system is much easier than cross-device identity resolution between anonymous and authenticated sessions at scale, and I know it. This is the single biggest gap between me and your ideal candidate.
